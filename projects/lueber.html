Core Sand Batch Mixer

Developed and validated a new horizontal batch mixer for core sand preparation, combining high throughput, compact packaging, homogeneous mixing and improved maintainability in a modular mechanical system.

Field

Mechanical engineering / Foundry equipment

Period

2017 – 2021

Focus

Product development / Mechanical design / Testing & optimization

Overview

Developing a new horizontal batch mixer for core sand preparation.

During my mechanical engineering studies, I worked part-time at Lüber and was responsible for developing a new batch mixer for core sand preparation. The objective was to build on the company’s existing know-how while introducing a fundamentally different mixing concept based on a horizontal mixing axis.

The existing mixers used a vertically aligned mixing shaft. This arrangement offered advantages such as simple shaft sealing and large mixing volumes, but it also resulted in longer mixing times, limited maintenance access and more residual material after discharge. A horizontal mixing axis allows the tool to lift the sand and use gravity to support both mixing and emptying.

My work covered the complete mechanical development process: gathering requirements, generating and evaluating concepts, detailed CAD design, prototype realization, internal testing with sand, and several design iterations focused on mixing performance, durability, manufacturability, cost and maintainability.

Key challenges

Combining high capacity, compact packaging and durability in an abrasive process.

The new mixer had to achieve a very high throughput while fitting within the same or a smaller installation envelope than the previous vertical-axis mixer. At the same time, the design had to improve serviceability and withstand highly abrasive, binder-coated sand and demanding cleaning conditions.

  • High mixing capacity within a compact installation space
  • Homogeneous mixing of sand and binder for consistent core-sand quality
  • Development of a new mixing tool geometry for efficient material circulation
  • Selection of wear-resistant and low-adhesion materials for abrasive, sticky media
  • Reliable sealing of the horizontal mixing shaft against sand, binder and cleaning water
  • Fast and complete discharge with minimal residual sand after each batch
  • Improved access for cleaning, inspection and maintenance
  • Design for cost-efficient manufacturing and straightforward assembly
  • Modular integration on different plant platforms and transport systems

Solution

A rotating horizontal mixing vessel with one opening for filling and discharge.

The central concept was a horizontal mixing vessel that can rotate around its axis. Instead of separate filling and discharge openings, the vessel uses one opening for both functions. For filling, the vessel rotates into a position where sand and binder can enter by gravity. After mixing, it rotates into the discharge position so the prepared sand can fall out through the same opening.

The horizontal mixing tool was designed to lift and circulate the material so that gravity contributes to the mixing process instead of continuously pulling the mixture away from the tool. During discharge, the mixing tool also assists in emptying the vessel, helping to reduce the amount of sand remaining after each batch.

Several prototypes were built and tested internally with sand. The first iteration validated the basic function and performance. The following iterations improved mixing quality, material selection, shaft sealing, manufacturability, cost and maintenance access. The mixer arm and wear components were refined for the highly abrasive and sticky sand-binder mixture.

The final design was developed as a modular subsystem that could be mounted on different platforms, including trolley-based and suspended transport arrangements. This made the mixer adaptable to different core sand preparation plants, where available space and material-flow layouts can vary considerably.

Reflection

What I took from the experience.

This project gave me early responsibility for a complete industrial product-development cycle, from requirements and concept generation through detailed mechanical design, prototyping, testing and iterative optimization. It showed me how strongly process performance, mechanical design, maintenance and manufacturing are connected in production equipment.

A key lesson was that improving a machine often requires rethinking its basic operating principle rather than only optimizing individual parts. Changing from a vertical to a horizontal mixing concept made it possible to use gravity as part of the process and opened new possibilities for mixing, discharge and maintenance.

The prototype tests were particularly valuable because they exposed effects that could not be fully predicted in CAD, including material flow, residual sand, wear behaviour, sealing and accessibility. These observations directly influenced the second and final design iterations.

The project also strengthened my understanding of material selection for harsh industrial environments. Abrasion resistance alone was not sufficient; surfaces also had to resist adhesion from the binder and remain suitable for repeated cleaning.

Overall, the development taught me to treat performance, durability, sealing, manufacturability, cost, assembly and serviceability as one connected engineering problem. It also showed the value of modular design when a subsystem must be integrated into customer-specific production plants.
